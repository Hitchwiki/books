# Residential Food Diving

[Food](Food "Food"){.wikilink} isn\'t a staple in residential bins to the same extent it is in commercial dumpsters, but an experienced and vigilant diver may be able to regularly glean groceries from their neighborhood apartments with practice. Many individuals and families will jettison the contents of their refrigerator, freezer and cupboards when moving, and if retrieved in time, these caches can be used by a canny scavenger.

# Methods

As always with residential [diving](Dumpster_Diving "diving"){.wikilink}, a would-be diver should brush up on the local [laws](Law "laws"){.wikilink} regarding scavenging, trash rummaging and trespass and should never go onto a single occupancy building\'s private property in the course of their hunt. As edible move-out caches are almost always disposed of the day before pick-up or on the last day of the month, a diver can scout for potential hot spots in the days before by keeping a lookout for bulkier items, such as mattresses and furniture, which are often disposed of much earlier. A diver can also scan viable dumpsters and how to approach them by making a habit of picking up and disposing of litter.

Once the diver has a good group of targets, they should approach them on the night before pick-up with the goal of spending as little time as possible on what might be construed as private property. This is best accomplished by learning how to *feel* individual bags for identifiable foodstuffs, such as weighty sealed cans or cold frozen bags, and then taking the bags as quickly as possible to another location for sorting. doing this reduces the risk of unwanted attention, as residents may feel threatened by somebody visibly sorting through their trash. It is ideal, when possible, to target residential dumpsters that are placed out on a \"public\" curb or sidewalk.

Once the bags are taken ready to sort, a diver should always have gloves, rags or towels and a diluted water/bleach solution on hand (for sanitizing packaging and containers. While residential diving for food can be very rewarding, it can also be considerably messier than commercial diving, and usable sealed containers may be spattered with loose food and refuse.

When looking at expiration dates, a diver should look for caches which all appear to have been sold at a similar time and which should be no more than a few weeks out of date. Food that has expired at vastly different time or which has been expired for more than a month may be indicative of somebody cleaning house rather than moving. Expiration dates on food which has been frozen is of less consequence, although it is still up to an individual diver as to what they feel comfortable in taking. When in doubt, one should always err on the side of caution. [^1]
