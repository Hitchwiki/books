# Guatemala

**Guatemala** is a country in [Central America](Central_America "Central America"){.wikilink}.

## Hitchhiking

Hitchhiking in Guatemala these days is relatively doable --- not too hard, though there are a few tricky areas. Most Guatemalans are familiar with the concept and refer to it as "pedir un jalón."

Near border crossings, it's usually easy to get a ride with large trucks, which can take you quite far --- even to the border of the next country, if that's your destination.

The most common rides you\'ll get are in the back of pickup trucks.

Just like elsewhere, it\'s harder to get rides near major cities, on fast highways, or on remote roads with little traffic.

Most roads in Guatemala have only one lane in each direction, and drivers often behave carelessly. The government reports over 1,000 accidents every month. Because of this, drivers may hesitate to stop on the roadside if they fear getting hit by other cars while picking someone up.

For that reason, your best option is to stand near speed bumps, where vehicles are forced to slow down.

It's also helpful to recognize the different types of public transportation in Guatemala so you can avoid unexpected expenses. You can find more about that in the Low-Cost Rides section.

## Public Transportation in Central America {#public_transportation_in_central_america}

Central America has many types of public transportation. If you plan to hitchhike, it\'s helpful to recognize them. If drivers see you with your hand raised and thumb out, they'll likely stop. However, you might need to be a bit assertive---either to get them to continue driving (in the best case) or to avoid accidentally boarding a ride that expects payment without you realizing it (in the worst case). Here's a short guide:

**Mototaxi / Mini** The classic three-wheeled vehicle. These go by many names: tuk-tuk, rickshaw, motocar, etc. Some have enclosed walls; others are completely open. They are very small and almost always operate as taxis. Minis are very similar but have four wheels and also very very small

**Colectivos** These are probably the hardest to recognize from a distance. Essentially, they are large vans with many seats. (See photo.)

**Chicken Bus** The easiest to spot---these are old U.S. yellow school buses that have been transformed for local transport. They usually feature wild paint jobs and are known for their reckless driving.

**Pickups/Camioneta** Pickup trucks with metal frames built onto the back are often used for short-distance transport. However, not all pickups with a rear structure are. Those with large metal frameworks are more likely to serve as buses.

**City Taxi** These are usually easy to identify. They look like typical taxis, often with a plastic sign on top and large "TAXI" lettering. They are typically white or yellow, sometimes with a checkered stripe on the sides.

**Illegal Taxi** Almost any private car can act as a taxi. Sometimes, someone just decides they're a taxi driver. This is why it's best to ask before getting into a car if it\'s a registered taxi. Fortunately, these are not very common. they are ilegal because they don\'t have taxi license.

Some private drivers who pick you up may also ask you to contribute toward fuel costs. To avoid misunderstandings, it's a good idea to ask before getting into the vehicle: "¿Es un ride, o eres taxi?" ("This is a free ride or are you a taxi?")

If you don't clearly agree on whether the ride is free (or negotiate a price) beforehand, drivers might later demand an inflated fare when you want to get out---by then, it\'s usually too late to say \"No.\" This applies not just to hitchhiking, but also to local taxis and other informal transport options.

The good news is that fares are typically very low---almost always under \$2 USD. Many hitchhikers choose to ride in one of these pick-ups or buses and pay the small fee. This is common practice among locals, many of whom are very poor. As a foreign traveler, it\'s normal to be expected to contribute a little---these small payments help local drivers offset the cost of owning and operating a vehicle.

So if you plan to hitchhike in Guatemala, expect a mix of free rides and low-cost contributions.

Additional Tip: As of 2024, the standard bus fare in Guatemala is Q10 per hour. You can use this as a rough guideline for calculating a fair contribution when riding with someone.

## Sleeping

Camping is not a very common practice for Guatemalans. Most Guatemalans will tell you that camping in a place remote with no guards is not safe. Many think that dangerous people are roaming in the middle of nowhere at the middle of night.

Practically Camping in Guatemala is generally safe and easy. I do recommend asking locals where you can camp, many times they will be very helpful and give you a good spot.

In many wonderful spots in nature where you are hidden you can camp and have a really chill night.

The exception is when you are close to big cities or borders. In places like Esquintla robbers might approach a tent if they see one.

Beside that Guatemala suffers from a large alcoholic population which tend to come an harass a tent if they see one.

Therefore, totally hidden spots, or guarded spots like someone's garden or a gas station if you get permission are where you should aim camping.
