# Bosnia and Herzegovina

## Hitchhiking

**Bosnia and Herzegovina** is generally good for hitchhiking as the people here are very warm and friendly. If you are from a western country, chances are good that the people you meet in the street will have friends or relatives living in your country (due to refugees who left Bosnia during the war). This makes for an easy conversation with locals (if you can communicate with them).

![](Hitchhiking_from_Sarajevo_to_Tuzla_and_Zenica.JPG.jpg "Hitchhiking_from_Sarajevo_to_Tuzla_and_Zenica.JPG.jpg"){width="200"}

Police won\'t give you any trouble here. In some places you may have to wait for quite some time, though. Indeed, Bosnia is quite poor and not so many people can afford traveling far away by car.

The roads are not in such a good quality (you will notice some small wood bridges, and your speed average may not excess 60 km/hour on most of the roads\...), there is just one short highway in Bosnia and Herzegovina. But Bosnia is a beautiful country, so get in a truck and enjoy the landscape.:)

Some car drivers will not understand the concept of hitchhiking, and private taxi is a major form of public transport. Therefore, make sure before you board a vehicle that the driver is not an informal taxi. Also make sure to keep an eye on the road - if the driver makes sudden turns or there are any other uncommon for usual driving changes in his behavior, it is likely that he is either a taxi driver or that he thinks he is doing good by bringing you to the local bus station. You therefore will have to be reiterative on stating that you travel exclusively by *autostop*.

Note that signs like \"20km\" could be misunderstood, as \"km\" is not interpreted as kilometers but as their currency (marka).

## Legality of Hitchhiking {#legality_of_hitchhiking}

Nothing in Bosnian law forbids hitchhiking (*autostop*), and on ordinary roads it is legal. Article 116(1) of the *Zakon o osnovama sigurnosti saobraćaja na putevima u BiH* keeps pedestrians, livestock and anything not counted as a motor vehicle off the *autoput*, and 116(2) adds motor vehicles that cannot exceed 40 km/h. Article 117(1) lets a driver stop on a motorway only on the areas off the carriageway that are specially built and marked for it, and 117(3) forbids U-turns and reversing, so a driver who passes you cannot come back. That leaves the marked rest areas and their petrol stations as the one place on the motorway where a lift can lawfully begin. Off the motorway, article 107(1) puts a pedestrian outside a settlement on the *left* edge of the carriageway facing the traffic, lit or in a reflective vest after dark --- which is where you stand and thumb.

## Dangers

Country has one of the most severe [land mine](land_mines "land mine"){.wikilink} problems in the world and accidents happen every year. Be aware that serious flooding in May 2014, shifted some land mines, and also shifted some notices marking unsafe or cleared areas. Walking off the road into the bushes on a spot you are not familiar with is not a good idea. Stick to paths, tire tracks and well grazed fields.

In the war houses were often rigged with mines as their owners fled during the war. If something looks \"too abandoned\", stay away from it. In general, any area which seems visited recently is safe to go, but any area which is all overgrown, and clearly not visited by anyone for a decade is not to be trusted until locals confirm it\'s safe.

There is an app which you can download called BH Mine Suspected Areas, which is a useful tool to check when you\'re considering getting off the beaten path.

Try to avoid hitching within cities as you will most likely attract a taxi.

## Personal Experiences {#personal_experiences}

<div class="hw-experience">

<div class="hw-exp-text">

I always reference Bosnia as being one of the easiest countries I ever hitchhiked through and a small country with plenty of character. I came in from Dubrovnik, Croatia to Mostar, onward to Sarajevo and then I continued towards Belgrade, Serbia. I found Bosnians very friendly and proud to see tourists in their country. Some people speak English because of the emigration during the war but even if they have no English they understand the concept of hitchhiking as many people here have hitchhiked before. Most towns like Mostar don\'t require any special spots or petrol stations just walk out of the town towards your next destination and find anywhere with space for cars to stop and stick your thumb out. Old Volkswagons make up most of the traffic in the countryside and I got collected by a taxi and public bus both for free. I also shared a back seat with a goat.
